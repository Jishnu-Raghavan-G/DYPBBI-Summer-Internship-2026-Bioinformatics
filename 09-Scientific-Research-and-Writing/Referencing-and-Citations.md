# Referencing and Citations

## Introduction

Scientific research depends on the ability to clearly identify and acknowledge the sources of information used during a study. Referencing and citation provide a connection between scientific statements and the original literature, databases, methods, or other sources from which the information was obtained.

During scientific research and writing, references are especially important when using information from research papers, databases, textbooks, websites, software documentation, or previously published studies.

Proper referencing helps maintain academic integrity, allows readers to verify information, and makes it easier to trace the development of scientific ideas.

---

## What Is a Citation?

A citation is a reference to the source of a particular piece of information, idea, result, method, or statement used in a scientific document.

For example, when discussing a biological mechanism based on a published research article, the corresponding article should be cited.

A citation generally appears within the main text, while the complete details of the source are provided in the reference list.

### Example

> Gene expression can vary considerably between healthy and disease conditions (Author et al., 2024).

The citation directs the reader to the corresponding entry in the reference list.

---

## What Is a Reference?

A reference contains the complete bibliographic information needed to identify and locate a source.

Depending on the reference style, it may include:

- Author name(s)
- Publication year
- Article title
- Journal or book title
- Volume
- Issue
- Page numbers
- DOI
- Publisher
- URL, where appropriate
- Access date, when required

### Example

```text
Author, A. A., & Author, B. B. (2024).
Title of the research article.
Journal Name, 12(3), 100–115.
https://doi.org/xxxxx
Citation vs Reference

Citation and reference are related but are not exactly the same.

Citation	Reference
Appears in the main text	Appears in the reference list
Usually short	Contains complete source information
Connects a statement to a source	Allows the reader to locate the source
Example: (Smith et al., 2024)	Full article details

A citation points to a source, while a reference provides the complete information about that source.

Why Referencing Is Important
1. Academic Integrity

References show that information obtained from other researchers has been properly acknowledged.

2. Avoiding Plagiarism

Using another person's ideas, words, figures, or results without appropriate acknowledgment can constitute plagiarism.

Proper citation helps distinguish the researcher's own work from information obtained from previous literature.

3. Supporting Scientific Claims

References provide evidence for scientific statements and help readers evaluate the basis of a conclusion.

4. Traceability

A reader should be able to follow a citation and locate the original source.

5. Building on Previous Research

Scientific research is cumulative. Researchers use existing literature to understand previous findings, identify gaps, and develop new research questions.

6. Improving Credibility

A well-referenced scientific document demonstrates that the researcher has consulted relevant literature and sources.

When Should a Source Be Cited?

A source should generally be cited when using:

Information obtained from a research paper
Published experimental results
Scientific theories or concepts from specific sources
Previously reported numerical data
Research methods developed by other researchers
Direct quotations
Paraphrased ideas
Figures or tables obtained from another source
Information from databases
Software or computational tools
Specific claims that require supporting evidence
Common Information That Usually Does Not Require Citation

Widely established facts or basic knowledge that are generally accepted within the field may not require a citation.

Examples include basic biological facts such as:

DNA contains genetic information.
Proteins are composed of amino acids.
Cells contain DNA.
The central dogma describes information flow from DNA to RNA to protein.

However, when in doubt, citing a reliable scientific source is generally safer than leaving a specific claim unsupported.

Experimental results

They are useful for:

Citation Styles

Different academic institutions and journals use different citation styles.

Common styles include:

APA
MLA
Chicago
Harvard
Vancouver
IEEE

Scientific and biomedical literature commonly uses author–date or numbered citation systems, depending on the journal or institution.

The most important rule is consistency: the same citation style should be followed throughout a document.

Author–Date Citation

In an author–date system, the author's surname and publication year appear in the text.

One Author
(Smith, 2024)
Two Authors
(Smith & Kumar, 2024)
Multiple Authors
(Smith et al., 2024)

The complete reference is then provided in the reference list.

Numbered Citation System

In a numbered system, each source is assigned a number.

Example
Gene expression analysis is widely used in disease research [1].

If another source is introduced:

Transcriptomic analysis can identify genes associated with disease conditions [2].

The reference list contains the corresponding numbered sources.

Direct Quotations

A direct quotation reproduces the exact wording of another source.

Direct quotations should be used sparingly in scientific writing.

When a quotation is necessary:

Preserve the original meaning.
Use quotation marks where appropriate.
Provide the citation.
Include page information when required by the citation style.

For scientific reports, paraphrasing is generally preferred over extensive direct quotation.

Paraphrasing

Paraphrasing means expressing information from a source using original wording while retaining the original meaning.

A proper paraphrase should not simply replace a few words with synonyms.

Example

Original idea:

Transcriptomic analysis can reveal changes in gene expression associated with disease.

Paraphrased:

Analysis of transcriptome-level data can be used to identify disease-associated changes in gene activity.

The source should still be cited because the underlying idea originated from another source.

Summarizing Sources

A summary presents the main ideas of a source in a shorter form.

For example, a research paper containing several experiments may be summarized by describing:

The research objective
The dataset or experimental system
The major methodology
The principal findings
The biological significance

The original paper should still be cited.

DOI

A DOI, or Digital Object Identifier, is a persistent identifier assigned to many academic publications.

A DOI makes it easier to locate a particular research article even if the journal's website structure changes.

Example
https://doi.org/10.xxxx/xxxxx

When available, the DOI is generally preferable to a long database or search URL in a formal reference.

PubMed and Database Records

Scientific articles may be discovered through resources such as PubMed or other scholarly databases.

When citing an article, the reference should normally describe the original publication rather than simply citing the search database.

For example, instead of treating a PubMed search-result page as the scientific source, the actual research article should be referenced.

Online sources should be cited carefully.

A useful web reference may include:

Organization or author
Page title
Website name

Access date, if required

For scientific work, primary literature and authoritative databases should generally be preferred over unsupported websites.

Referencing Figures and Tables

Figures and tables taken or adapted from another source require appropriate acknowledgment.

Example
Figure 1. Overview of the proposed workflow. Adapted from Smith et al. (2024).

A figure reproduced exactly may require additional permission depending on the source's copyright conditions.

When creating a new figure based on information from several sources, the relevant sources should still be cited.

Referencing Software

Computational research should acknowledge important software and analytical platforms used during the work.

For example:

Cytoscape was used for visualization and analysis of biological interaction networks.

The corresponding software or publication should be cited when required.

Similarly, when a specific computational method is central to the analysis, the original method or software publication should be referenced.

A reference manager can reduce manual work, but it does not guarantee that imported metadata is correct.

Citation Consistency

Every citation style should be applied consistently throughout the document.

For example, if the document uses numbered citations:

[1]
[2]
[3]

The same system should not suddenly be replaced by:

(Smith, 2024)

unless the document's required style allows or requires such a change.

Reference List vs Bibliography

A reference list generally contains sources that were cited in the document.

A bibliography may contain sources that were consulted during preparation, even if they were not directly cited, depending on the required academic style.

The exact distinction varies between institutions and citation systems.

Common Referencing Mistakes
1. Missing Citations

A scientific claim is presented without identifying its source.

2. Incomplete References

Important information such as the DOI, journal, or page range is missing.

3. Incorrect Author Information

Author names are entered incorrectly.

4. Inconsistent Formatting

Different references use different formatting styles without justification.

5. Citing Secondary Sources Instead of Primary Sources

A researcher may cite a review when the original research paper would be more appropriate.

6. Incorrect DOI or URL

An incorrect identifier prevents readers from locating the source.

7. Citation–Reference Mismatch

A citation appears in the text but has no corresponding reference entry, or a reference appears in the list but is never cited.

8. Over-Citation

Almost every sentence is cited even when the information is common knowledge or several consecutive statements are clearly supported by the same source.

9. Under-Citation

Important claims, methods, or ideas from other researchers are presented without adequate references.

Good Referencing Practice

A strong scientific referencing workflow can be organized as follows:

Identify information requiring a source
            ↓
Find the original or authoritative source
            ↓
Record complete bibliographic information
            ↓
Use the source in the scientific document
            ↓
Add the appropriate in-text citation
            ↓
Add or generate the reference entry
            ↓
Verify citation and reference consistency

Primary Sources and Secondary Sources
Primary Sources

Primary sources present original research or original data.

Examples:

Original research articles
Experimental studies
Original datasets
Dataset-associated publications
Secondary Sources

Secondary sources interpret or summarize existing research.

Examples:

Review articles
Systematic reviews
Textbooks
Educational resources

Both can be useful, but the appropriate source depends on the claim being made.

Research Paper Review and Citations

When reviewing a research paper, references should be used to distinguish:

What the paper reports
What previous studies reported
What the researcher concludes
What the reviewer independently interprets

For example:

The authors reported an increase in the expression of specific inflammatory genes.

This statement should be supported by the paper being reviewed.

If the reviewer then connects the finding to another biological mechanism discussed in a different publication, that second source should also be cited.

Scientific Writing Principle

A useful principle is:

Cite the source of the information, not merely a source that happens to contain similar information.

The closer the source is to the original evidence, the easier it is for the reader to verify the claim.

Practical Checklist

Before finalizing a scientific report, check:

[ ] All important external claims are cited
[ ] Original research papers are used where appropriate
[ ] Database resources are acknowledged
[ ] Software/tools are cited where required
[ ] Figures and tables have appropriate attribution
[ ] In-text citations match the reference list
[ ] Reference formatting is consistent
[ ] Author names are correct
[ ] Publication years are correct
[ ] DOI/identifiers are correct
[ ] No unnecessary citations have been added
[ ] References can be traced to reliable sources
