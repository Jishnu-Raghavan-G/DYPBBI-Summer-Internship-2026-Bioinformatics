# Scientific Ethics

Scientific ethics refers to the principles and standards that guide responsible conduct in scientific research.

Ethical research requires researchers to be honest, transparent, responsible, and respectful when conducting experiments, handling data, interpreting results, and communicating scientific findings.

## 1. Importance of Scientific Ethics

Scientific research produces knowledge that may influence future research, healthcare, technology, and society.

Therefore, researchers have a responsibility to ensure that their work is:

- Honest
- Accurate
- Transparent
- Responsible
- Reproducible
- Respectful of people, animals, and biological materials

3. Research Integrity

Research integrity means conducting and reporting research according to accepted scientific and ethical standards.

Researchers should:

Record observations accurately
Report results honestly
Describe methods truthfully
Acknowledge limitations
Give appropriate credit to contributors
Maintain research records

Research integrity can be represented as:

Accurate Data
     +
Honest Analysis
     +
Transparent Reporting
     +
Proper Attribution
     ↓
Research Integrity
4. Honesty in Research

Researchers should report observations and results honestly, even when the results do not support the original hypothesis.

For example:

Hypothesis
    ↓
Experiment
    ↓
Unexpected Result
    ↓
Report the Actual Result

A researcher should not alter the data simply to obtain an expected outcome.

5. Fabrication

Fabrication means making up data or results that were never obtained.

Example:

Actual experiment:
10 samples analyzed

Fabricated report:
20 samples analyzed

Fabricated data cannot be considered scientific evidence because the reported observations did not actually occur.

6. Falsification

Falsification involves manipulating research materials, processes, data, or results so that the research record no longer accurately represents what was performed or observed.

Examples may include:

Altering measurements
Manipulating experimental conditions without disclosure
Selectively changing results
Removing inconvenient observations without justification

Fabrication and falsification seriously undermine scientific integrity.

7. Plagiarism

Plagiarism involves presenting another person's words, ideas, or intellectual contributions as one's own without appropriate attribution.

Examples include:

Copying text without citation
Using another researcher's ideas without acknowledgment
Presenting another person's analysis as original
Excessive copying with minor changes

A good workflow is:

Read Source
    ↓
Understand Information
    ↓
Write in Your Own Words
    ↓
Cite Original Source
8. Proper Attribution

Researchers should give appropriate credit to people whose work contributed to their research.

Sources that may require acknowledgment include:

Research papers
Databases
Software
Datasets
Research methods
Figures
Previously published ideas

Proper attribution allows readers to identify the origin of information.

9. Authorship

Authorship should reflect meaningful contributions to the research and publication.

Contributors may be involved in:

Research design
Data collection
Data analysis
Interpretation
Manuscript preparation
Substantial revision

Authorship practices vary between disciplines and journals, so researchers should follow the relevant institutional and journal guidelines.

10. Conflict of Interest

A conflict of interest may occur when a researcher's personal, professional, financial, or other interests could potentially influence—or appear to influence—the research.

Researchers should disclose relevant conflicts according to the requirements of the institution, journal, or study.

Potential Conflict
       ↓
Disclosure
       ↓
Transparency

Disclosure does not automatically mean that research is invalid. It allows readers to evaluate the research with the relevant information.

11. Responsible Data Management

Research data should be handled responsibly.

Important practices include:

Keeping accurate records
Preserving original data
Documenting processing steps
Protecting sensitive information
Maintaining appropriate backups
Preventing unauthorized modification

A useful workflow is:

Raw Data
   ↓
Secure Storage
   ↓
Documented Processing
   ↓
Analysis
   ↓
Results
12. Data Privacy

Some biological and biomedical datasets may contain sensitive information.

Researchers working with such data should follow applicable:

Institutional policies
Ethical approvals
Data-access requirements
Privacy regulations
Consent requirements

Personal or identifiable information should not be unnecessarily exposed.

13. Ethics in Human Research

Research involving human participants requires careful consideration of participant rights and welfare.

Important principles may include:

Informed consent
Privacy
Confidentiality
Risk minimization
Voluntary participation
Ethical review

The exact requirements depend on the type of research and applicable institutional and legal frameworks.

14. Informed Consent

Informed consent means that participants are provided with relevant information about a study so that they can make an informed decision about participation.

Depending on the study, information may include:

Purpose of the research
Procedures
Potential risks
Potential benefits
Data handling
Voluntary nature of participation

Participants should have an appropriate opportunity to ask questions and make a voluntary decision.

15. Ethics in Animal Research

Research involving animals requires attention to animal welfare and ethical standards.

Important considerations may include:

Scientific justification
Minimizing unnecessary harm
Appropriate housing and care
Use of suitable procedures
Trained personnel
Ethical review

The principles commonly summarized as the 3Rs are:

Replacement
Reduction
Refinement
Replacement

Use alternatives to animals where scientifically appropriate.

Reduction

Use the minimum number of animals necessary to obtain scientifically meaningful information.

Refinement

Modify procedures to minimize pain, distress, or suffering.

16. Ethical Use of Biological Materials

Biological materials should be obtained, handled, stored, and disposed of according to applicable safety and ethical requirements.

Examples include:

Cell cultures
Microorganisms
Human samples
Animal tissues
Genetic material

Researchers should follow institutional laboratory procedures and relevant regulations.

17. Responsible Statistical Analysis

Ethical research also requires appropriate statistical analysis.

Researchers should avoid:

Deliberately choosing a method only because it produces a desired result
Misrepresenting statistical significance
Ignoring relevant assumptions
Selectively reporting favorable analyses
Hiding important limitations

A responsible approach is:

Research Question
      ↓
Study Design
      ↓
Appropriate Statistical Method
      ↓
Analysis
      ↓
Accurate Reporting
18. Selective Reporting

Selective reporting occurs when researchers present only selected findings while omitting relevant results in a way that gives a misleading impression.

For example:

10 analyses performed
       ↓
2 favorable results
       ↓
Only 2 reported

This can create a misleading interpretation of the overall evidence.

Researchers should report findings transparently according to the study and reporting requirements.

19. Image and Figure Integrity

Scientific figures should accurately represent the underlying data.

Researchers should not manipulate figures in ways that create a misleading scientific impression.

For example:

Original Data
      ↓
Appropriate Visualization
      ↓
Scientific Figure

Any legitimate image processing should be consistent with the requirements of the relevant journal or institution and should not misrepresent the underlying evidence.

20. Ethical Use of Computational Tools

Bioinformatics research often involves databases, software, scripts, and computational resources.

Responsible practice includes:

Citing databases
Citing software where appropriate
Documenting analysis methods
Recording relevant parameters
Respecting database access conditions
Maintaining reproducible workflows

For example:

Dataset
   ↓
Database
   ↓
Software
   ↓

   ↓
Citations + Documentation
21. Ethical Use of Artificial Intelligence

AI-based tools can assist with:

Literature organization
Programming assistance
Data processing
Drafting
Brainstorming
Language improvement

However, researchers remain responsible for the accuracy and integrity of their work.

Important practices include:

Verify generated information
Check scientific claims
Protect confidential data
Follow institutional policies
Disclose AI use when required
Do not present generated material as verified evidence without checking it

AI tools should support scientific work rather than replace scientific responsibility.

22. Reproducibility and Transparency

Transparent research allows other researchers to understand how conclusions were reached.

Important information may include:

Data sources
Experimental procedures
Analysis methods
Software
Parameters
Statistical methods
Limitations

    ↓

    ↓

23. Ethical Scientific Communication

Scientific findings should be communicated without exaggerating the evidence.

For example:

Evidence:
Gene A was associated with disease severity.

Appropriate:
Gene A may have relevance to disease-associated
biological processes.

Overstated:
Gene A causes the disease.

The strength of scientific language should match the strength of the evidence.

24. Peer Review

Peer review is a process in which scientific work is evaluated by other researchers with relevant expertise.

It can help identify:

Methodological problems
Missing information
Unsupported claims
Statistical issues
Interpretation problems

Researchers should also treat unpublished manuscripts and peer-review information confidentially according to the applicable journal policies.

26. Common Ethical Problems
Fabrication

Making up data or results.

Falsification

Manipulating research materials, processes, or data in a misleading manner.

Plagiarism

Using another person's work without appropriate attribution.

Improper Authorship

Giving or receiving authorship without appropriate contribution.

Undisclosed Conflicts

Failing to disclose relevant conflicts of interest.

Selective Reporting

Reporting only selected findings in a misleading manner.

Data Mismanagement

Poor handling, storage, documentation, or protection of research data.

28. Key Takeaways
Scientific ethics promotes responsible and trustworthy research.
Researchers should report observations and results honestly.
Fabrication involves making up data.
Falsification involves misleading manipulation of research materials, processes, or data.
Plagiarism involves using another person's work without appropriate attribution.
Research contributions should be appropriately acknowledged.
Relevant conflicts of interest should be disclosed.
Research data should be handled securely and responsibly.
Human research requires attention to consent, privacy, and participant welfare.
Animal research should follow appropriate ethical standards and the 3Rs.
Statistical analysis should be performed and reported responsibly.
Scientific figures should accurately represent the underlying evidence.
Computational resources, datasets, and software should be appropriately documented and cited.
Researchers remain responsible for verifying information generated or assisted by computational and AI tools.
Transparent reporting and reproducibility strengthen scientific research.
Conclusion

Scientific ethics is fundamental to maintaining the reliability and credibility of scientific research.

Honest data handling, appropriate attribution, responsible analysis, transparent reporting, respect for research participants and biological materials, and careful interpretation of evidence help ensure that scientific knowledge can be trusted and built upon by others.
