# Plagiarism and Originality

Plagiarism is the use of another person's words, ideas, data, figures, or other intellectual contributions without appropriate acknowledgment.

Originality means presenting one's own understanding, analysis, interpretation, and writing while properly acknowledging information obtained from other sources.

## 1. What Is Plagiarism?

Plagiarism occurs when someone presents another person's work or ideas as their own without appropriate attribution.

Plagiarism can involve:

- Written text
- Ideas
- Research findings
- Data
- Figures
- Tables
- Computer code
- Images
- Other intellectual contributions

A simple example is:

```text
Source
  ↓
Copy information
  ↓
Present as your own
  ↓
Plagiarism
2. Why Plagiarism Is a Problem

Plagiarism violates academic and scientific integrity.

It can:

Deny credit to the original creator
Misrepresent the source of information
Reduce trust in academic work
Lead to academic penalties
Damage research credibility

Scientific work depends on accurate attribution and transparency.

3. Common Forms of Plagiarism
Direct Plagiarism

Direct plagiarism occurs when text is copied from a source without appropriate quotation and citation.

Original Source
      ↓
Copied Word-for-Word
      ↓
No Attribution
      ↓
Direct Plagiarism
Mosaic Plagiarism

Mosaic plagiarism involves taking phrases or structures from different sources and combining them with minor changes without proper attribution.

Changing a few words is not sufficient to make copied material original.

Idea Plagiarism

Using another person's original idea or interpretation without giving appropriate credit can also constitute plagiarism.

Self-Plagiarism

Self-plagiarism refers to reusing one's own previously submitted or published work inappropriately without disclosure or appropriate attribution.

The rules surrounding reuse can vary between institutions and publishers.

4. Plagiarism in Scientific Research

Scientific research often involves information from many sources.

For example:

Research Papers
      +
Databases
      +
Books
      +
Software Documentation
      ↓
Research Work

The original sources should be acknowledged when their information, ideas, data, or other contributions are used.

5. Paraphrasing

Paraphrasing means expressing information from a source using your own wording while maintaining the original meaning.

A good process is:

Read Source
    ↓
Understand the Meaning
    ↓
Take Notes
    ↓
Close / Move Away from Source
    ↓
Write in Your Own Words
    ↓
Compare Meaning
    ↓
Cite the Source

Simply replacing individual words with synonyms is not proper paraphrasing.

6. Example of Paraphrasing

Suppose a source states:

Gene expression analysis can be used to identify
molecular changes associated with disease.

A properly paraphrased version could be:

Analysis of gene-expression patterns can help
investigate molecular alterations linked to disease.

The original source should still be cited because the underlying idea came from the source.

7. Direct Quotations

Sometimes the exact wording of a source may need to be reproduced.

In such cases:

Use quotation marks where appropriate
Clearly identify the source
Provide the required citation

Direct quotations should generally be used sparingly in scientific writing.

Scientific writing usually emphasizes synthesis and paraphrasing rather than extensive quotation.

8. Citation and Attribution

Citation is an important method of avoiding plagiarism.

A basic workflow is:

Information / Idea
        ↓
Identify Original Source
        ↓
Use Information
        ↓
Cite Source
        ↓
Include Reference

Sources may include:

Research papers
Books
Scientific databases
Websites
Software
Datasets

The exact citation format depends on the institution, journal, or project requirements.

9. What Usually Requires Citation?

Citation is generally appropriate when using:

Published research findings
Specific scientific claims derived from sources
Research methods developed by others
Data obtained from external sources
Figures or images created by others
Specific ideas or interpretations
Information that is not your own original contribution
10. Common Knowledge

Information considered common knowledge within a field may not always require a citation.

For example:

DNA carries genetic information.

However, the decision depends on the context and academic requirements.

When uncertain, citing a reliable source is generally safer than leaving an important scientific claim unsupported.

11. Originality in Scientific Writing

Original scientific writing does not mean that every scientific idea must be newly discovered.

Instead, originality can involve:

Your own explanation
Your own organization of information
Your own analysis
Your own interpretation
Your own comparison of studies
Your own computational workflow

A useful distinction is:

Existing Knowledge
       +
Your Analysis / Interpretation
       +
Proper Attribution
       ↓
Original Scientific Work
12. Originality in Bioinformatics

Bioinformatics projects often use existing datasets and established tools.

For example:

Public Dataset
      ↓
Your Processing
      ↓
Your Analysis
      ↓
Your Visualization
      ↓
Your Interpretation

Using a public dataset does not mean the entire project lacks originality.

The analysis and interpretation can represent your own work, while the dataset and external resources should be appropriately acknowledged.

13. Code and Plagiarism

Plagiarism can also occur with computer code.

Examples include:

Copying code without attribution when attribution is required
Presenting another person's program as your own
Removing copyright or license information
Reusing code contrary to its license

For GitHub projects, it is important to check the repository's license and documentation before reusing code.

14. Images, Figures, and Tables

Images, figures, and tables obtained from external sources may also require attribution.

A useful workflow is:

External Figure
      ↓
Check Usage Rights
      ↓
Check License / Permission
      ↓
Use Appropriately
      ↓
Provide Attribution

When creating a new figure from external data, the underlying data source should still be acknowledged.

15. Avoiding Accidental Plagiarism

Plagiarism is not always intentional.

It can happen when research notes are poorly organized.

A useful note-taking method is:

SOURCE:
Author / Paper

DIRECT QUOTE:
"Exact words..."

MY NOTES:
Your own understanding

MY INTERPRETATION:
Your analysis

CITATION:
Reference information

Clearly separating copied text from personal notes reduces the risk of accidentally using source wording as original writing.

16. Reference Management

Reference-management tools can help organize sources.

A reference library may contain:

Paper
├── Authors
├── Title
├── Journal
├── Year
├── DOI
└── Notes

Keeping references organized throughout a project makes final citation easier.

17. Plagiarism Detection

Plagiarism-detection software can compare text against existing sources.

However, a similarity score does not automatically prove plagiarism.

Similarity can occur because of:

Technical terminology
Standard scientific phrases
Properly quoted material
Reference lists
Common methodological descriptions

Therefore, similarity reports should be interpreted carefully.

18. Academic Integrity

Academic integrity involves conducting academic and research work honestly.

Important principles include:

Honesty
Originality
Proper attribution
Accurate reporting
Responsible collaboration
Transparency

A simple framework is:

Original Work
      +
Proper Attribution
      +
Honest Reporting
      ↓
Academic Integrity
19. Plagiarism and Research Reports

When preparing a research or internship report, avoid copying large sections from:

Websites
Research papers
Previous reports
Course material
Other students' work

Instead:

Learn
 ↓
Understand
 ↓
Organize
 ↓
Write
 ↓
Cite

The report should reflect your own understanding of the work.

20. Plagiarism and GitHub Documentation

GitHub repositories should also maintain proper attribution.

For example, if a project uses:

An external dataset
A published method
Existing code
A scientific software package
A database

the relevant source or license should be documented.

A README can include:

## Data Sources

Dataset:
[Dataset name]

Source:
[Database / Repository]

## References

[List relevant scientific sources]
21. Responsible Use of AI Tools

AI tools can assist with:

Brainstorming
Language improvement
Code assistance
Organization
Drafting

However, researchers remain responsible for the final work.

Important practices include:

Verify factual claims
Check references
Review generated code
Avoid submitting unverified generated content
Protect confidential information
Follow institutional AI policies
Disclose AI use when required

AI assistance does not remove the responsibility to maintain academic integrity.

23. Common Mistakes
Copying and Changing a Few Words

Replacing individual words while keeping the original sentence structure can still constitute inappropriate copying.

Forgetting Citations

Ideas and findings obtained from sources should be appropriately attributed.

Poor Note-Taking

Mixing copied text with personal notes can lead to accidental plagiarism.

Reusing Previous Work Without Disclosure

Previously submitted or published material may have reuse restrictions.

Ignoring Software and Data Licenses

External code and datasets may have specific conditions for reuse.

25. Key Takeaways
Plagiarism involves using another person's work or ideas without appropriate attribution.
Direct copying without proper citation is plagiarism.
Changing a few words from a source does not automatically make the writing original.
Proper paraphrasing requires understanding and independently expressing the original idea.
Sources should be cited when their findings, ideas, data, methods, or other contributions are used.
Research datasets, software, code, figures, and images may also require attribution or license compliance.
Good note-taking helps prevent accidental plagiarism.
Similarity-detection scores should be interpreted carefully rather than treated as automatic proof of plagiarism.
Originality can come from analysis, interpretation, organization, and methodology even when existing scientific resources are used.
AI-assisted work still requires human verification and adherence to academic policies.
Proper attribution and transparent reporting are essential components of scientific integrity.
Conclusion

Plagiarism prevention is an essential part of responsible scientific writing and research.

Researchers regularly build upon existing knowledge, datasets, methods, and software. The goal is therefore not to avoid using previous work, but to use it responsibly by understanding the source, contributing original analysis or interpretation, and providing appropriate attribution.
